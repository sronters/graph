\textbf{Abstract.}
Modern identity and access management (IAM) exposure can emerge from combinations of individually ordinary relationships among users, groups, roles, workloads, pipelines, policies, and cloud resources. This study asks whether whole-identity graph analysis detects and explains modeled multi-hop exposure more effectively than direct-permission and privileged-identity audits. GraphTrust compiles provider-normalized IAM evidence into a directed heterogeneous capability graph, performs bounded path analysis, produces an auditable Zero Trust Risk Index (ZTRI), and recommends raw permission changes using cost-aware graph optimization. We evaluate five detection methods on SEIB-2026, a deterministic synthetic-enterprise benchmark with three organizational profiles, paired clean and injected variants, fixed final seeds, hard negatives, and truth withheld until inference completes. Primary endpoints are risky-starting-identity recall, scenario recall, exact-path recall, ranking quality, false-positive burden, runtime, and peak memory. GraphTrust substantially outperforms direct and privileged-only baselines for transitive exposure and improves ranking over an untyped reachability baseline, but it does not improve recall over that untyped baseline; therefore typed semantics provide prioritization and explanation rather than universal detection superiority. Counterfactual remediation verifies proposed changes after application and checks protected business workflows. Results support a bounded claim: whole-identity reachability closes important audit blind spots, while typed risk modeling makes the resulting path volume more reviewable. Synthetic structure, ordinal weights, static snapshots, and bounded search limit external validity.
\end{minipage}}

\section{Research question and significance}

\textbf{Question.} To what extent does typed, whole-identity graph analysis improve detection, ranking, explanation, and remediation of multi-hop paths to critical cloud resources compared with four precisely defined IAM-audit baselines?

Zero Trust Architecture rejects implicit trust based on network location and requires access decisions to consider subjects, assets, resources, and policy context \citep{nist2020,nist2023}. IAM reviews that stop at direct grants can miss a low-privilege identity whose group membership, role assumption, service impersonation, or deployment authority creates transitive reachability. Existing work already demonstrates graph-based attack-path and IAM analysis; Principal Mapper and BloodHound are prominent examples \citep{pmapper,bloodhound}. GraphTrust therefore does not claim to invent IAM graphs. Its contribution is a platform-neutral, truth-separated benchmark and controlled comparison that combines typed path ranking, decomposed risk explanations, and workflow-constrained remediation.

We preregistered three hypotheses: (H1) whole-identity analysis increases recall of planted transitive scenarios over direct and privileged-only audits; (H2) typed semantics improve top-ranked relevance over untyped traversal; and (H3) cost-aware cuts reduce exposure with fewer or lower-cost raw IAM changes while preserving protected workflows.

\section{Method}

\subsection{SEIB-2026 benchmark and experimental matrix}

SEIB-2026 generates three enterprise profiles---SaaS scale-up, regulated finance, and global hybrid---rather than uniformly random graphs. Each graph contains departments, correlated group and role assignment, human and non-human identities, policies, repositories, pipelines, secrets, and multi-cloud resources. Twelve scenario families inject documented dangerous paths and hard negatives. Paired variants share the same clean base graph, so changes in detection are attributable to the injected condition rather than unrelated topology.

The final matrix crosses 3 profiles, 5 held-out seeds, 3 variants (clean, injected-low, injected-mixed), and 5 methods: 225 immutable runs per scale. Every run stores dataset/configuration hashes, git revision, environment, predictions, paths, scores, warnings, timings, and checksums. Truth is first joined after inference. Clean graphs measure unmatched-finding burden; detection endpoints are estimated on injected graphs. Reported uncertainty uses paired graph-level bootstrap intervals (10,000 resamples), Friedman omnibus tests, and Holm-corrected paired Wilcoxon follow-ups. Reachability is potential authorization exposure, not proof of successful exploitation.

Small graphs use a 250,000 global path cap. Retained medium diagnostics at 250,000, 30,000, 5,000, and 1,000 exceeded the practical runtime envelope, so the final medium matrix is a bounded scalability test: depth 6, a 100-path global cap, and one path per source and source--target pair. Large launches use 10,000. Truncation is persisted per run; medium recall is not interpreted as exhaustive enterprise path recall.

\subsection{Graph semantics, scoring, and baselines}

Raw IAM evidence is compiled into effective capability edges while retaining the semantic rule and source evidence identifiers. Conditions use three states (true, false, unknown); unknown conditions are retained conservatively and labeled. Paths are simple, bounded to eight transitions, and limited by explicit per-source and global expansion caps. For path $p$ ending at asset $t$,
\[
R(p)=C(t)\,S(p)\exp\!\left[-\sum_{e\in p}\bigl(-\log x_e+\tau_e+\kappa_e\bigr)\right],
\]
where $C$ is normalized asset criticality, $S$ starting-control weakness, $x_e$ relative exploitability, and $\tau_e,\kappa_e$ are typed-transition and condition penalties. Values are ordinal ranking parameters, not breach probabilities. ZTRI combines critical reach, best path, path diversity, blast radius, and control weakness; every component remains visible.

Baselines are B0 direct (one-hop grants), B1 privileged-only (typed traversal from pre-labeled privileged starts), B2 untyped graph (unit-cost reachability), and B3 native scope (direct, group, and provider-native relationships). This avoids the inaccurate claim that commercial IAM products inspect only direct permissions. AWS Access Analyzer, for example, uses automated reasoning for supported resource and policy scopes \citep{awsanalyzer}.

\subsection{Counterfactual remediation}

Removable raw relationships receive modeled business costs; mandatory edges and required workflows are protected. Four methods---degree greedy, risk greedy, weighted minimum cut, and iterative CP-SAT path hitting set---receive the same target. The verifier removes proposed raw edges, removes every effective transition supported by them, reruns bounded reachability, and then checks capability-specific protected workflows. A plan is labeled verified only if the specified blocked-path target and all workflow constraints hold. Recommendations never modify live permissions.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Detection and ranking}

The final small-scale matrix contains \SmallRunCount\ checksum-verified runs. On injected graphs, GraphTrust achieved mean risky-identity recall \GraphtrustRiskyRecall\ and scenario recall \GraphtrustScenarioRecall, compared with \DirectRiskyRecall/\DirectScenarioRecall\ for direct audit, \PrivilegedRiskyRecall/\PrivilegedScenarioRecall\ for privileged-only audit, \NativeScopeRiskyRecall/\NativeScopeScenarioRecall\ for native scope, and \UntypedRiskyRecall/\UntypedScenarioRecall\ for untyped traversal. Thus H1 is supported against the narrow audit baselines but not against untyped whole-graph reachability.

The more informative separation appears in ranking. GraphTrust reached NDCG@10 \GraphtrustNdcgTen, versus \UntypedNdcgTen\ for untyped traversal and \NativeScopeNdcgTen\ for native scope. This supports H2's narrower claim: typed conditions and transition costs help surface relevant planted paths near the top even when raw reachability recall ties. GraphTrust exact-path recall was \GraphtrustExactPathRecall\ versus \UntypedExactPathRecall\ for untyped traversal; the paired difference was \VsUntypedExactPathRecallDifference\ (95\% CI \VsUntypedExactPathRecallCI). On clean controls, GraphTrust produced \GraphtrustCleanBurden\ unmatched paths per 1,000 identities, close to untyped traversal at \UntypedCleanBurden. An unmatched synthetic path is not necessarily a real false alarm, but the volume shows why reachability must be ranked and reviewed rather than treated as an incident list.

Paired differences make the boundary explicit. Against untyped traversal, the risky-identity-recall difference was \VsUntypedRiskyRecallDifference\ (95\% bootstrap CI \VsUntypedRiskyRecallCI), while the NDCG@10 difference was \VsUntypedNdcgTenDifference\ (95\% CI \VsUntypedNdcgTenCI). The five-method Friedman tests gave $p=\FriedmanRiskyRecallP$ for risky-identity recall and $p=\FriedmanNdcgTenP$ for NDCG@10. Inferential tests use the enterprise graph, not each reported path, as the independent paired unit.

\subsection{Remediation and scalability}

On the preregistered injected-mixed verification graph, weighted min-cut proposed \CutChanges\ raw IAM changes at modeled cost \CutCost. Applying those changes counterfactually reduced enumerated relative exposure by \CutExposureReduction\ and preserved all protected workflows; verification status was \CutVerified. Greedy alternatives used different cost/change trade-offs, showing that ``minimal'' depends on the stated objective and constraints. The initial large launch also exposed a recursion-dependent quality check; replacing it with iterative traversal was necessary before large graphs could be generated reproducibly. This negative engineering result is retained because scale claims must include preprocessing, not only the final path search.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}

Synthetic structural realism does not establish real-world prevalence. Injected truth covers known scenario families and may favor methods aligned with those families; conversely, unlabeled plausible paths lower measured precision. Risk weights and removal costs are ordinal assumptions. Provider normalization cannot capture every policy exception, runtime session, deny interaction, or temporal condition. Bounded top-$K$ search may omit paths, so truncation and search completeness are retained per finding. Critical-asset labels and protected workflows are generated proxies for organizational decisions. The three profiles improve heterogeneity but are not a representative sample of companies. Finally, reachability describes potential exposure, not attacker intent, exploit success, or incident probability.

\section{Conclusion and impact}

GraphTrust supports a precise conclusion rather than a universal superiority claim. Whole-identity reachability reveals transitive paths that direct and privileged-only audits systematically miss in SEIB-2026. Typed semantics do not increase recall over untyped whole-graph traversal, but they materially improve top-ranked relevance, provenance, and explanation. Counterfactual remediation translates choke points into raw IAM changes and verifies exposure reduction plus workflow preservation. Thus GraphTrust complements provider-native IAM tools; it is not autonomous enforcement evidence.

\section*{Appendix A: Reproducibility and experiment matrix}
The repository pins Python dependencies, provides Docker and Compose definitions, and stores a Colab notebook that processes one large profile at a time with resume checkpoints. The complete final matrix is $3\times2\times5\times3\times5=450$ planned small/medium runs, plus three large profile launches at one held-out seed. Immutable run IDs derive from the dataset checksum, resolved configuration, git commit, and method. A separate traceability validator rejects missing or corrupt artifacts before report generation.
